# Overview
"Classical conditioning represents a form of associative learning in which an individual comes to associate one stimulus for another. This process occurs through repeated pairings of the two stimuli, leading to a learned response that was not originally present." _Dan Biscayne_


Classical conditioning, also known as associative learning theory, was discovered by Ivan Pavlov. He made this discovery while studying the digestive processes in dogs. The experiment was to surgically implant fistulas in the dogs' salivary glands as a way to collect and measure saliva in dogs. Pavlov introduced a variety of stimuli to dogs, including the ringing of bells. He observed that dogs would begin to salivate at the sound of the bell even when no food was present. This led to the research of classical conditioning, where a stimulus that produces no response can be conditioned to produce a conditioned response.

## For Dogs
For us in dog training, this means pairing a stimulus, such as food, with a marker, such as a clicker or verbal communication, can elicit the same response as food.

# Fundamental Concepts
- Environmental factors can stimulate a response from the dogs.
- A neutral stimulus that elicits no response from a dog can be conditioned to do so when paired closely with a naturally stimulating source.
- The transformation of the neutral stimulus into a conditioned stimulus highlights the significance of stimuli in shaping behavioral responses.
- We can use variations of the conditioned stimulus to generalize the marker.
- Discrimination involves the ability to distinguish between multiple stimuli and respond selectively.
- Close association is pivotal for effective conditioning and emphasizes the importance of timing in influencing learning outcomes.
- Once a stimulus is conditioned, it can be used to evoke similar responses when conditioning a new unconditioned stimulus.
- The closer in space and time the unconditioned stimulus is presented after the conditioned stimulus the stronger the association will be between them.
- Higher contingency creates a heightened expectancy and anticipation of the natural stimulus.

## Reinforcement
- Reinforcement refers to the process of strengthening a particular association between a conditioned stimulus and a conditioned response.
- Reinforcement can occur through reinforcement or punishment:
    - Reinforcement: Increases the likelihood of the behavior happening again
        - Positive reinforcement - Providing a positive stimulus the dog wants
        - Negative reinforcement - Taking away an aversive stimulus that makes the dog uncomfortable
    - Punishment: Decreases the likelihood of the behavior happening again. Either decreases in intensity or happens less often than before.
        - Positive Punishment - Providing an aversive stimulus to the dog
        - Negative Punishment - Taking a positive stimulus away from the dog
- Consistent reinforcement yields more robust associations.

## Primary vs Secondary Stimulus
A primary stimulus is a stimulus that has innate biological significance, such as:
    - Food
    - Water
    - Pain

Whereas secondary stimuli acquire their significance through association with a primary stimulus.
   - We can think of the dog's prey drive. Chasing or killing prey is associated with the primary stimulus of food.
   - Escape and avoidance behaviors may be associated with pain.

## Behavior Modification
- Habituation - repeated exposure to a specific stimulus can lead to habituation and a decreased response
    - If you're afraid of other dogs, repeated exposure can lead to a decrease in fear of other dogs.
        - The dog is afraid of being hurt by the other dog. Through gradual exposure to other dogs where no pain, and thus no primary stimulus, is experienced, the stimulus of seeing dogs becomes desensitized. This leads to extinction of the response, and the dog develops a neutral response to seeing other dogs.
- Sensitization - repeated exposure to a specific stimulus can lead to increased response
    - Seeing people and then barking at them, and seeing that the barking has an effect on people, can lead to an amplified reaction to seeing people.

# Classical Conditioning vs. Other Conditioning Models
- B.F. Skinner's Operant Conditioning, which deals with the interaction between behavior and consequences.
- Aversive Conditioning, which involves the pairing of a particular stimulus with an unpleasant experience to reduce the frequency of undesired behavior. It works by addressing maladaptive behaviors by creating negative associations
    - This is our "no" marker

# Definitions
- **Unconditioned Stimulus**: refers to a stimulus that naturally triggers a response without requiring prior learning.
- **Unconditioned Response**: refers to a response that is naturally occurring, such as salivation, or dopamine release.
- **Conditioned Stimulus**: is a previously neutral stimulus that through association with the unconditioned stimulus provokes a **conditioned response**. 
- **Conditioned Response**: just like any unconditioned response, except it is provoked from a conditioned stimulus. 
- **Extinction**: when a conditioned response from a conditioned stimulus diminishes over time when repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus.
- **Higher-order conditioning**: where a conditioned stimulus is used to create further associations with other new neutral stimuli
- **Generalization**: Similar stimuli can elicit the conditioned response with varying degrees of strength. 

# Behavior Modification
Individuals can be exposed to anxiety-provoking stimuli while being presented with relaxation techniques and classical conditioning to reduce fear and avoidance. Classical conditioning is instrumental in helping people address phobias, addictive behaviors, and traumatic experiences.

# Assumptions of Classical Conditioning
- Environmental stimuli can elicit an innate response
- Pairing of conditioned and unconditioned stimuli must occur in close temporal proximity
- Extinction of a conditioned response occurs when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus
- Generalization is true
- Higher-order conditioning is true
