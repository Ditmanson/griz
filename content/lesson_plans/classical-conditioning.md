# Overview
"Classical conditioning represents a form of associative learning in which an individual comes to associate one stimulus for another. This process occurs through repeated pairings of the two stimuli, leading to a learned response that was not originally present." _Dan Biscayne_

Classical conditioning, also known as associative learning theory, was discovered by Ivan Pavlov. He made this discovery while studying the digestive processes in dogs. The experiment was to surgically implant fistulas in the dogs' salivary glands as a way to collect and measure saliva in dogs. Pavlov introduced a variety of stimuli to dogs, including the ringing of bells. He observed that dogs would begin to salivate at the sound of the bell even when no food was present. This led to the research of classical conditioning, where a stimulus that produces no response can be conditioned to produce a conditioned response.

## For Dogs
For us in dog training, this means pairing a stimulus, such as food, with a marker, such as a clicker or verbal communication, can elicit the same response as food.

# Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Unconditioned Stimulus (US) | A stimulus that naturally triggers a response without requiring prior learning. |
| Unconditioned Response (UR) | A naturally occurring response, such as salivation or dopamine release. |
| Conditioned Stimulus (CS) | A previously neutral stimulus that, through association with the US, provokes a conditioned response. |
| Conditioned Response (CR) | A response provoked by a conditioned stimulus — functions like an unconditioned response, but learned. |
| Extinction | A conditioned response diminishes over time when the CS is repeatedly presented without the US. |
| Generalization | Stimuli similar to the CS can elicit the CR, with varying degrees of strength. |
| Discrimination | The ability to distinguish between multiple stimuli and respond selectively. |
| Higher-order conditioning | An established conditioned stimulus is used to create further associations with new neutral stimuli. |
| Habituation | Repeated exposure to a specific stimulus leads to a decreased response. |
| Sensitization | Repeated exposure to a specific stimulus leads to an increased response. |
| Primary stimulus | A stimulus with innate biological significance (e.g. food, water, pain). |
| Secondary stimulus | A stimulus that acquires significance through association with a primary stimulus. |

# Fundamental Concepts
- Environmental factors can stimulate a response from the dog.
- A neutral stimulus that elicits no response can be conditioned to do so when paired closely with a naturally stimulating source.
- The closer in space and time the unconditioned stimulus is presented after the conditioned stimulus, the stronger the association between them.
- Higher contingency (the CS reliably predicting the US) creates a heightened expectancy and anticipation of the natural stimulus.
- We can use variations of a conditioned stimulus to generalize the marker.
- Once a stimulus is conditioned, it can be used to evoke similar responses when conditioning a new stimulus (see Higher-order conditioning).

# Reinforcement & Punishment
Reinforcement strengthens the association between a stimulus and a response; punishment weakens it. Consistent application yields more robust associations.

| Quadrant | Effect on Behavior | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Positive Reinforcement | Increases likelihood of the behavior happening again | Providing a positive stimulus the dog wants |
| Negative Reinforcement | Increases likelihood of the behavior happening again | Taking away an aversive stimulus that makes the dog uncomfortable |
| Positive Punishment | Decreases likelihood of the behavior happening again | Providing an aversive stimulus to the dog |
| Negative Punishment | Decreases likelihood of the behavior happening again | Taking a positive stimulus away from the dog |

# Primary vs. Secondary Stimulus

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary stimulus | Has innate biological significance | Food, water, pain |
| Secondary stimulus | Acquires significance through association with a primary stimulus | Prey drive (chasing/killing prey is associated with the primary stimulus of food); escape and avoidance behaviors (associated with pain) |

# Habituation & Sensitization

| Process | Effect | Dog Example |
|---|---|---|
| Habituation | Repeated exposure to a stimulus decreases the response | A dog afraid of other dogs, given gradual exposure where no pain (no primary stimulus) is experienced, becomes desensitized to seeing dogs — the response extinguishes and the dog develops a neutral reaction. |
| Sensitization | Repeated exposure to a stimulus increases the response | A dog that barks at people, and for whom that barking has an effect on people, develops an amplified reaction to seeing people. |

# Classical Conditioning vs. Operant Conditioning
Classical conditioning is about stimulus-stimulus association — a neutral stimulus comes to predict a naturally significant one. This is a distinct model from B.F. Skinner's **Operant Conditioning**, which deals with the interaction between a behavior and its consequences (see the Reinforcement & Punishment table above).

> **Aversive Conditioning is not a third, parallel model.** Pairing a behavior with an unpleasant consequence to reduce its frequency — addressing a maladaptive behavior by creating a negative association — is **Positive Punishment**, a technique within Operant Conditioning. This is what our "no" marker is.

# Assumptions of Classical Conditioning
- Environmental stimuli can elicit an innate response.
- Pairing of conditioned and unconditioned stimuli must occur in close temporal proximity.
- Extinction of a conditioned response occurs when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus.
- Generalization is true.
- Higher-order conditioning is true.

# Applications & Use Cases (Human Psychology)
- Educational practices
- Psychological interventions — pairing anxiety-provoking stimuli with relaxation techniques to reduce fear and avoidance
- Behavioral therapies — addressing phobias, addictive behaviors, and traumatic experiences
- Also applied in disciplines such as artificial intelligence, human education, and therapy more broadly
